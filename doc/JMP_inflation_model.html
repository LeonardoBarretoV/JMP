Debt-Financed Fiscal Stimulus, Heterogeneity, and Welfare

Model with Endogenous Inflation, and Counterfactual Results
Leonardo Barreto
Wharton · September 2026
Abstract This document restates the model of the paper with the exogenous inflation assumption removed. Inflation is now determined inside the model by a sticky-wage block: a continuum of labor unions set nominal wages subject to a quadratic adjustment cost, households supply hours under proportional rationing, and a monetary authority follows a Taylor rule. Sections 1–3 are the model, equilibrium and calibration; Sections 4–5 report the counterfactual results under the extension, together with a decomposition of the one channel that the extension adds and the flexible-wage model does not have.

1. Model

I build an open economy heterogeneous agent model in the spirit of Bewley (1977), İmrohoroğlu (1989), Aiyagari (1994), and Huggett (1996). Time is discrete and indexed by t\in\{1,2,\dots\}. There is no aggregate uncertainty. Inflation is determined endogenously: nominal wages are sticky, so the price level is pinned down jointly by a wage Phillips curve, a monetary policy rule and the Fisher equation. The economy is populated by domestic and foreign households, final good firms, a continuum of labor unions, a monetary authority, and a government. Domestic households consume final goods, supply labor at hours set by their union, face time-varying idiosyncratic income risk, and self-insure by saving and borrowing in capital and long-term nominal bonds. Foreign households also demand domestic bonds. Firms produce the final good using a constant-returns-to-scale technology with capital and labor as inputs. The government pays out transfers to households and finances its expenditures through labor and capital income taxes as well as bond issuance. I consider a perfect foresight transition sequence between an initial stationary equilibrium with a given stock of government debt and a new stationary equilibrium with a higher debt-to-GDP ratio following a one-time zero-probability shock.

1.1 Domestic Households

Preferences. The economy is populated by a unit continuum of ex-ante identical households indexed by i\in[0,1]. Household preferences are given by the following Epstein–Zin (1989) utility function:

V_{it}= \left\{(1-\beta)x_{it}^{1-1/\xi} + \beta \left[\mathbb{E}\left( V_{it+1}^{1-\gamma}\right) \right]^{\frac{1-1/\xi}{1-\gamma}} \right\}^{\frac{1}{1-1/\xi}},

where \mathbb{E} denotes the expectation operator over idiosyncratic states, \beta\in(0,1) is the discount factor, \xi is the elasticity of intertemporal substitution, \gamma is the risk aversion coefficient, and x is the Greenwood–Hercowitz–Huffman (1988) composite of consumption and labor:

x_{it} = c_{it} - \theta \frac{n_{it}^{1+1/\nu}}{1+1/\nu},

where c_{it} denotes consumption of the final good, sold at nominal price P_t, and n_{it} is labor supplied. The parameter \nu controls the Frisch elasticity of labor supply and \theta is a labor disutility parameter. The GHH composite plays a larger role here than in the flexible-wage version: because the marginal disutility of work is separable from consumption, the steady-state hours profile is independent of assets, which is what makes the proportional rationing scheme below internally consistent.

Idiosyncratic income risk. Let z_{it}\geq 0 be the idiosyncratic component of household i's income. It follows a Markov process with ergodic mean normalized to one, evolves according to a time-varying transition matrix \Omega_t, and has an unemployment state in which z_{it}=0. The U-U transition probability is P^{uu}_t \equiv \mathrm{Prob}(z_{it}=0\mid z_{it-1}=0) and the E-E transition probability is P^{ee}_t \equiv \mathrm{Prob}(z_{it}>0\mid z_{it-1}>0). These probabilities capture the labor market dynamics since 2020.

Hours under sticky wages. This is the first point at which the model departs from the flexible-wage version. Nominal wages are set by unions, not by households, and hours are rationed proportionally: every employed household works the same fraction of its own steady-state hours. Let \tilde n(z) denote the hours a household with productivity z works in the initial stationary equilibrium, and let s_t be a common, economy-wide rationing index. Then

n_{it} \;=\; s_t\,\tilde n(z_{it}),

with \tilde n(0)=0, so unemployed households supply no labor. Households take n_{it} as given and choose only consumption and savings. Aggregate effective labor is

N_t \;=\; s_t \sum_{z} \Lambda_t(z)\, z\, \tilde n(z),

where \Lambda_t(z) is the date-t marginal distribution of productivity.

Proportional rationing is not an innocuous normalization. Under flexible wages, with the GHH composite and the HSV tax scheme below, the household's intratemporal condition delivers hours proportional to \tilde n(z) with a factor that is common across z: the productivity term z^{1-\tau} cancels exactly. That common factor is

s^{\mathrm{flex}}_t \;=\; \left[\frac{\tau_t^n}{\tau^n_{ss}}\left(\frac{w_t}{w_{ss}}\right)^{1-\tau}\right]^{\frac{\nu}{1+\tau\nu}},

so the family of hours profiles spanned by the rationing scheme contains every flexible-wage allocation. Setting s_t = s^{\mathrm{flex}}_t recovers the flexible-wage model exactly, which nests the original paper as a limiting case and provides a direct numerical check on the extension.

Budget and borrowing constraints. Households receive W_tz_{it}n_{it} in labor earnings, where W_t is the nominal wage per efficiency unit. Labor income is taxed progressively according to a Heathcote–Storesletten–Violante (2017) scheme \mathcal{T} depending on a rate \tau^n_t. Households receive unemployment benefits P_tT^u_t when unemployed and lump-sum transfers P_tT^s_t if z_{it}<\bar z. They save in long-term nominal bonds and capital; with a_{it}=k_{it}+q_{t-1}b_{it} and (1+r_t) the equalized gross return for t>0:

c_{it} + a_{it+1} \leq w_{t} z_{it} n_{it} + (1+r_t) a_{it} - \mathcal{T}(w_{t} z_{it} n_{it}) + T_{t}^u \mathbf{1}_{z_{it}=0} + T^s_{t} \mathbf{1}_{z_{it}<\bar z}, \quad t>0
c_{i0} + a_{i1} \leq w_0 z_{i0} n_{i0} + (1+r_0^k) k_{i0} + (1+r_0^b) q_{-1} b_{i0} - \mathcal{T}(w_{0} z_{i0} n_{i0}) + T_{0}^u \mathbf{1}_{z_{i0}=0} + T^s_{0} \mathbf{1}_{z_{i0}<\bar z}

where w_t\equiv W_t/P_t. A standard borrowing constraint applies: a_{it+1}\geq \underline{a}.

Lockdown constraints. With rationing in place, the labor restriction becomes a ceiling on the rationing index rather than a household-level constraint: when \mathrm{lock}_t=1,

s_t \;=\; \min\!\left\{\Gamma^n,\; s^{\mathrm{flex}}_t\right\}, \qquad c_{it} \;=\; \Gamma^c\, c^{ss}_i,

so hours are capped at \Gamma^n\tilde n(z) and the consumption restriction binds with equality at a common fraction \Gamma^c of each household's steady-state consumption. This follows Fourakis (2024) and lets the model match the observed drop in output and consumption in 2020Q2. Whether the ceiling is the binding branch is not an assumption but an equilibrium object, and it is verified at every solution: if s^{\mathrm{flex}}_t fell below \Gamma^n the household and government blocks would book different hours and the solution would be invalid. Section 4 reports the realized margin for each column.

Recursive formulation. Let s\equiv\{z,a\} be the idiosyncratic state. Hours no longer appear as a choice variable:

V_{t}(z,a) = \max_{c,\,a^\prime} \left\{(1-\beta)x^{1-1/\xi} + \beta \left[\mathbb{E}\left( V_{t+1}(z^\prime, a^\prime)^{1-\gamma}\right) \right]^{\frac{1-1/\xi}{1-\gamma}} \right\}^{\frac{1}{1-1/\xi}}

subject to the budget constraints above, n = s_t\,\tilde n(z), a^\prime\geq \underline{a}, and the lockdown restriction.

1.2 Foreign Households, Firms

Foreign households demand government bonds, the only form of international capital flows: B_{t+1}^\star = \Psi_t q_t^{-\chi}, where \chi>0 governs the elasticity of foreign demand with respect to the bond price and \Psi_t is an exogenous shifter. The final good is produced competitively with F(K_t,L_t) = Z K_t^\alpha L_t^{1-\alpha}; firms hire labor at w_t, rent capital at \hat r^k_t, and pay a proportional tax \tau^k_t on capital factor payments, so F_K = (1+\tau^k_t)\hat r^k_t and F_L = w_t.

1.3 Labor Unions and Wage Setting

This block is new relative to the published version of the model and is what makes inflation endogenous.

Structure. There is a continuum of labor tasks. Each task is organized by a union that represents all households, and every household supplies the same proportion of every task, which is what delivers proportional rationing. Union j sets the nominal wage for its task and faces a quadratic cost of adjusting it, as in Rotemberg (1982). The structure follows Erceg–Henderson–Levin (2000) and its heterogeneous-agent implementation in Auclert–Rognlie–Straub (2024). Unions maximize the average utility of their members net of the adjustment cost. I express the adjustment cost in utility units; it therefore does not appear in the goods resource constraint, so measured output and consumption are unaffected by it.

Wage inflation. Define nominal wage inflation as

1+\pi^w_t \;\equiv\; \frac{W_t}{W_{t-1}} \;=\; (1+\pi_t)\,\frac{w_t}{w_{t-1}},

which follows from w_t = W_t/P_t and 1+\pi_t = P_t/P_{t-1}. This is the bridge between the real wage the firm block prices and the nominal rigidity the union faces: it is the only place inflation enters the real side of the model.

The wage Phillips curve. Let \lambda_{it} be the marginal utility of the GHH composite, which for the Epstein–Zin recursion is

\lambda_{it} \;=\; \frac{\partial V_{it}}{\partial x_{it}} \;=\; (1-\beta)\, V_{it}^{1/\xi}\, x_{it}^{-1/\xi},

and let \rho_{it} be the marginal retention rate implied by the HSV scheme,

\rho_{it} \;=\; (1-\tau)\,\tau^n_t\,\bigl(w_t z_{it} n_{it}\bigr)^{-\tau},

so \rho_{it} w_t z_{it} is the marginal after-tax real wage. The union's first order condition delivers the Rotemberg wage Phillips curve

\boxed{\;(\pi^w_t - \bar\pi)(1+\pi^w_t) \;=\; \frac{\kappa_w}{w_{ss}\mathcal{Y}_{ss}}\,\Xi_t \;+\; \beta\,(\pi^w_{t+1} - \bar\pi)(1+\pi^w_{t+1})\;}

where \bar\pi is the inflation target, \kappa_w governs wage stickiness, and \Xi_t is the utility-weighted labor wedge

\Xi_t \;=\; \int_i \lambda_{it}\, n_{it} \Bigl[\;\underbrace{\theta\, n_{it}^{1/\nu}}_{\text{marginal disutility}} \;-\; \underbrace{\rho_{it}\, w_t\, z_{it}}_{\text{marginal after-tax wage}}\;\Bigr] di.

The wedge is the gap between the marginal rate of substitution between leisure and consumption and the marginal after-tax real wage, weighted by each household's marginal utility and hours. When the wedge is zero, households are at their intratemporal optimum and wage inflation equals its target; when households would like to work more than the union has contracted at the going wage, the wedge is negative and wage inflation falls below target. Substituting the rationing scheme and separating the terms that depend on s_t gives the form used in computation,

\Xi_t \;=\; s_t\Bigl[\theta\, s_t^{1/\nu}\,\mathcal{D}_t - w_t\,\mathcal{Y}_t\Bigr], \qquad \mathcal{D}_t \equiv \int_i \lambda_{it}\,\tilde n(z_{it})^{1+1/\nu} di, \qquad \mathcal{Y}_t \equiv \int_i \lambda_{it}\,\rho_{it}\, z_{it}\, \tilde n(z_{it})\, di,

where \mathcal{D}_t and \mathcal{Y}_t are built from the steady-state hours profile \tilde n(z) and all dependence on s_t is explicit. Both integrals run over employed households only; the unemployed contribute nothing, and \rho_{it} is not defined at z=0. The normalization by w_{ss}\mathcal{Y}_{ss} makes \kappa_w a dimensionless elasticity. In the initial stationary equilibrium s=1 and \theta=1, so \Xi_{ss} = \mathcal{D}_{ss} - w_{ss}\mathcal{Y}_{ss} = 0: the wedge vanishes and the Phillips curve reduces to \pi^w = \bar\pi, as it must.

1.4 Monetary Policy

A monetary authority sets the nominal interest rate i_t between t and t+1 according to a Taylor (1993) rule,

i_t \;=\; i^\star + \phi_\pi\,(\pi_t - \bar\pi) + \varepsilon^m_t, \qquad \phi_\pi > 1,

where i^\star is the intercept and \varepsilon^m_t a monetary policy shock. The nominal rate is linked to the real return by the Fisher equation,

1 + i_t \;=\; (1+r_{t+1})(1+\pi_{t+1}).

Consistency of the rule with the terminal stationary equilibrium requires that the intercept satisfy Fisher at that point: with \pi=\bar\pi, r=r_f and \varepsilon^m\to0,

i^\star \;=\; (1+r_f)(1+\bar\pi) - 1.

This restriction matters for the counterfactuals. Any experiment that changes the terminal real rate r_f changes the right-hand side, and therefore changes i^\star + \varepsilon^m_\infty. Section 5 isolates the effect of that shift.

Determinacy and the backward recursion. Combining the rule and Fisher and solving for \pi_t,

\pi_t \;=\; \bar\pi + \frac{(1+r_{t+1})(1+\pi_{t+1}) - 1 - i^\star - \varepsilon^m_t}{\phi_\pi}.

Given the path of real returns and the terminal condition \pi_T=\bar\pi, this determines the entire inflation path by backward recursion. The map contracts at rate (1+r)/\phi_\pi \approx 0.67 precisely because \phi_\pi>1, so the Taylor principle delivers a unique bounded solution constructively rather than merely ruling out indeterminacy. Two features shape the results below. First, i^\star and \varepsilon^m_t enter only through their sum, so the intercept and the shock are not separately identified: they are one instrument, not two. Second, the fixed point under a constant C \equiv i^\star + \varepsilon^m is

\pi^\ast \;=\; \bar\pi + \frac{(1+r_f)(1+\bar\pi)-1-C}{\phi_\pi - 1 - r_f},

which equals \bar\pi if and only if the Fisher restriction holds. A deviation d in the rule constant therefore shifts inflation along the transition by -d/(\phi_\pi-1-r_f), an object used directly in Section 5.

1.5 Asset Structure and Government

There are two assets: a long-term nominal bond and capital. Let \pi_t\equiv P_t/P_{t-1}-1, now an endogenous object. As in Hatchondo–Martínez (2009), a bond issued in t promises (\bar i+\vartheta)(1-\vartheta)^{j-1}/(1+\pi_{t+j}) units of the final good in t+j, where \bar i makes the bond price one in the initial steady state and \vartheta controls maturity. Returns are

1 + r_{t}^b = \frac{ (\bar{i} + \vartheta)/(1+\pi_{t}) + [(1-\vartheta)/(1+\pi_{t})] q_{t}}{q_{t-1}}, \qquad 1 + r_{t}^k = 1 + \hat{r}_t^k - \delta,

and no-arbitrage equates them for t>0. Because the bond is nominal and long-term, the first expression is the channel through which endogenous inflation redistributes: an inflation path below what was expected raises the real value of coupon payments and transfers resources to bondholders. This channel is absent when inflation is exogenous and fixed at \bar\pi, and it accounts for the largest single discrepancy between the extension and the published results.

The government taxes labor income through the Heathcote–Storesletten–Violante schedule

\mathcal{T}(w_{t} z_{it} n_{it}) = w_t z_{it} n_{it} - \tau_t^n (w_t z_{it} n_{it})^{1-\tau},

where \tau^n_t is a retention level: a lower \tau^n_t means a heavier tax burden, and \tau^n_t>1 is a net labor subsidy. It is the residual instrument balancing the budget each period. The government also levies \tau^k_t on gross capital returns (negative during the pandemic, a subsidy to firms), pays unemployment insurance T^u_t and lump-sum transfers T^s_t, and consumes G_t. Its budget constraint is

\tau_t^k \hat{r}_t^k K_t + q_t \ell_t + \int_i\mathcal{T}(w_{t} z_{it} n_{it})\, di = \left(\frac{\bar{i} + \vartheta}{1+\pi_t}\right) B_t + \int_i T_{t}^s \mathbf{1}_{z_{it}<\bar z}\, di + \int_iT_{t}^u \mathbf{1}_{z_{it}=0}\, di + G_t

with \ell_t = B_{t+1}-[(1-\vartheta)/(1+\pi_t)]B_t. Inflation now enters the government's problem endogenously through the real coupon (\bar i+\vartheta)/(1+\pi_t): the debt is nominal, so the inflation path determines how much of the stimulus is financed by erosion of the real value of outstanding debt rather than by taxation.

Market clearing. Labor market clearing is N_t = L_t; asset market clearing is \mathcal{A}_{t} = q_{t-1}(B_{t} - B_t^\star) + K_{t} for t>0; and the goods resource constraint is F(K_t, N_t) = \mathcal{C}_t + I_t + G_t + NX_t with I_t\equiv K_{t+1}-(1-\delta)K_t and NX_t\equiv q_tB^\star_{t+1}-(1+r_t)q_{t-1}B^\star_t. The wage adjustment cost does not enter the resource constraint.

2. Equilibrium

Definition 1 (Competitive Equilibrium). Given an initial distribution of household assets and idiosyncratic productivities, a sequence of transition matrices \{\Omega_t\}, a government policy composed of a fiscal policy \{B_t,\tau^n_t,\tau^k_t,T^s_t,T^u_t,G_t\}, a monetary policy \{\phi_\pi,\bar\pi,i^\star,\varepsilon^m_t\} and a lockdown policy \{\mathrm{lock}_t\}, an equilibrium is a sequence of aggregates \{\mathcal{A}_t,B^\star_t,K_t,N_t,Y_t,\mathcal{C}_t,s_t\} and prices \{q_t,r^b_t,r^k_t,w_t,\pi_t,\pi^w_t,i_t\} such that:
  • Given prices, transition matrices, the rationing index and government policy, households optimize
  • Given prices and government policy, firms optimize
  • Unions optimize: the wage Phillips curve holds at every date, with the wedge evaluated at the equilibrium distribution
  • The monetary authority follows the Taylor rule, the Fisher equation holds, and the intercept satisfies the Fisher restriction at the terminal stationary equilibrium
  • Hours are consistent with proportional rationing in both the household and the government block
  • The sequential government budget constraint is satisfied
  • The no-arbitrage condition is satisfied for t>0
  • Markets clear

A stationary equilibrium is an equilibrium in which all sequences are constant over time. Relative to the flexible-wage version, the equilibrium system gains one equation per date, the Phillips curve, and one unknown per date, the rationing index s_t; inflation is then recovered from the backward recursion. The bolded conditions are the new ones. The fifth is not redundant: the household block and the government's tax base must book the same hours, and it is checked at every date in the numerical solution, since a violation would mean the labor tax revenue implied by the household distribution differs from what the budget constraint records.

The monetary shock path \{\varepsilon^m_t\} is not a free parameter. It is recovered as the residual of the Taylor rule evaluated at the solved real rate path and the observed inflation series, so that the baseline transition reproduces measured inflation by construction:

\varepsilon^m_t \;=\; \bigl[(1+r_{t+1})(1+\pi^{\mathrm{data}}_{t+1}) - 1\bigr] \;-\; i^\star \;-\; \phi_\pi\bigl(\pi^{\mathrm{data}}_t - \bar\pi\bigr).

This is the natural analogue of the exogenous inflation assumption in the published model: rather than imposing the inflation path directly, I impose the monetary shocks that generate it, which lets the model speak to how much of observed inflation the fiscal and foreign blocks can account for on their own. The answer, below, is very little.

4. Counterfactual Results

I consider four counterfactuals, each holding the monetary rule and the monetary shocks fixed and changing one element of the fiscal or external environment. Welfare is measured as a consumption-equivalent (CE) gain at the announcement date relative to the pre-stimulus stationary equilibrium.

The monetary rule is held fixed across columns. Because i^\star and \varepsilon^m_t enter the recursion only as their sum, the object that must be held fixed is the rule constant C_t \equiv i^\star + \varepsilon^m_t. Two of the four counterfactuals move the terminal real rate and therefore the value the constant must take in the far tail. I hold C_t at its baseline value for t \leq 600 quarters and let it revert to the counterfactual's own Fisher-consistent value thereafter. Since \beta^{600}=7.3\times10^{-4}, this has no effect on announcement-date welfare, while keeping the terminal condition \pi_T=\bar\pi exactly consistent. Under this specification the rule constant is numerically identical across all four columns at every date t=2,\dots,600.

Reading Table 3. On every average, the extension reproduces the published levels closely: the overall mean is within 0.02 to 0.27 percentage points across all five columns, Middle within 0.33, Rich within 0.28, and Poor within 0.33. The ranking and sign pattern of the published table survive: removing the capital subsidies raises average welfare while making the rich substantially worse off; a weaker foreign demand for government debt lowers welfare throughout; a stimulus package consisting of checks alone is far more progressive; and matching the CBO debt path raises welfare relative to the realized path.

The exception is the Overall minimum row, which I do not regard as reconciled. That statistic is the CE gain of a single (a,z) cell with positive mass, so it is far more sensitive to the specification than any average. The extension puts it at −5.383 in the baseline against −1.77 in the published model, and at −10.063 against −3.72 under lower foreign demand, while matching to 0.08 under no capital subsidies. The direction is intuitive — with sticky wages, the households worst hit by the rationing of hours have no margin on which to adjust — but I have not isolated the mechanism, and the row should be read as an artifact of the extension rather than a result.

What the extension adds. Table 4 reports the objects the flexible-wage model cannot produce. The interpretation of the last column requires care. Because \varepsilon^m_t is recovered so that the baseline matches measured inflation, the peak deviation of about 6.8 percentage points is the deviation in the data, not an endogenous model response. What the extension generates on its own is the difference across columns, and that is small: at most +0.15 percentage points at 2022Q2, under lower foreign demand. Absent monetary shocks, the fiscal and external blocks of this model move inflation very little — which is the central quantitative finding of the extension.

The CBO debt path is the only counterfactual that raises welfare at the top, and it does so by more than it raises welfare in the middle — a pattern the next section attributes to a specific channel.

5. The Neutral-Rate Channel

The two counterfactuals that change the terminal real rate — lower foreign demand and the CBO debt path — activate a channel that does not exist in the flexible-wage model, and it is worth isolating because it accounts for the largest discrepancy between the extension and the published results.

The channel. A counterfactual that raises the terminal real rate r_f raises the Fisher-consistent rule constant. Holding the monetary shocks fixed, a rise d in that constant lowers inflation along the transition by

d\pi \;=\; -\,\frac{d}{\phi_\pi - 1 - r_f}.

Since government debt is nominal and long-term, a permanently lower inflation path raises the real value of coupon payments, transferring resources to bondholders, while the heavier labor taxation needed to service the debt falls on households who hold little of it. The channel is thus redistributive by construction, and it operates in the direction of the wealthy.

Measurement. I switch the channel off by subtracting d from the monetary shock over the transition, which restores the rule constant to its baseline value while leaving the terminal condition intact — the post-600 specification of Section 4. Comparing that solution with one in which the constant is allowed to rise isolates the channel. The measured shift in inflation matches the closed form to 0.0008 percentage points for the CBO column and 0.0001 for lower foreign demand, flat across four hundred quarters, while real rates move by at most 0.006 percentage points. The channel is, to a very good approximation, purely nominal.

Result. For the CBO column the channel is large and sharply redistributive. With it operating, the top bin gains +1.609 percentage points relative to the baseline; switching it off removes 0.914 of that, so the channel accounts for 57% of the entire gain accruing to the top decile. Over bins three through eight it works in the opposite direction, and removing it converts losses of 0.28–0.53 percentage points into gains of 0.06–0.43. Without the channel, all four welfare groups gain in the CBO column, as they do in the published table, and the top group matches the published value to 0.006 percentage points.

For lower foreign demand the channel is proportionally similar but small in absolute terms, at most 0.14 percentage points against a counterfactual effect of −1.19 to +0.25: the terminal rate moves by only 5 basis points there, against 34 for the CBO path. Indeed the channel's welfare contribution scales almost exactly linearly in d: dividing the CBO contribution by the ratio of the two shifts, 6.51, predicts the lower-foreign contribution to within one to three percent in every group. That linearity is useful, since it means the channel can be priced for any further experiment that moves the terminal steady state from the size of the shift alone.

Interpretation. The published, flexible-wage model has no such channel: with inflation exogenous and constant, a change in the terminal real rate cannot redistribute through the nominal value of government debt. The extension therefore does not overturn the paper's conclusions, but it does identify an additional force that operates whenever a fiscal experiment moves the neutral rate, and it shows that force to be quantitatively first-order for the debt-path counterfactual specifically. A reader of the published table should treat the CBO column's distributional pattern as the one most sensitive to the assumption that inflation is exogenous.
